# MiniProject 3 — Validation Reflection

Comparing the two validation logs showed that disagreements were concentrated in fields requiring interpretation rather than simple transcription. The clearest example was `HasRecovered`: labels such as “Partially” and “Barely” do not fit the Coding Guide’s binary Yes/No categories, so the decision depended on whether activity resumed after a qualifying inactivity gap. The activity-pattern disagreements also showed that short-term fluctuation can coexist with a clear long-term decline; commit counts across the full timeline helped distinguish an overall downward trajectory from irregular activity.

Jackson Murphy and Tristan Lopez discussed the disagreements and agreed on final adjudicated values. The resolution process used the Coding Guide to normalize nonstandard recovery labels, describe recovery triggers with concrete post-gap evidence, select a dominant activity-pattern category, and choose the two most representative recent-commit themes. The original pre-discussion agreement statuses remain unchanged in the validation log so the independent coding results can still be distinguished from the final adjudications.

A central lesson was the importance of matching each field to its evidence source. Project-gap summaries are useful for explaining what happened after long pauses, while `RecentThemes` is based on the most recent commits. Keeping these evidence types distinct makes the coding more reproducible and reduces the risk of inferring a recent theme from activity that occurred much earlier. Consistent category names and separators also make the results easier to compare across projects.

If repeating the validation, I would first apply the field definitions mechanically—especially the three-month recovery threshold and six-month current-status cutoff—then inspect the specific commits supporting borderline cases, and finally compare the independent codings. This sequence would make the reasoning behind each decision explicit and help the partners resolve differences efficiently. Overall, the exercise demonstrated that a shared Coding Guide improves consistency, but that careful evidence selection and discussion are still important when repository histories support more than one plausible interpretation.
